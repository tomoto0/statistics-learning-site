## Statistical and financial-learning sources

- [American Statistical Association: Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf) and [Wasserstein, Schirm & Lazar (2019), “Moving to a World Beyond p < 0.05”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00031305.2019.1583913) inform the p-value module. A p-value is not the probability that a hypothesis is true, does not measure effect size or practical importance, and does not establish causality; avoid binary threshold ritual and report methods and evidence transparently.
- [St. Louis Fed FRED Blog: Measuring uncertainty and volatility with FRED data](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/blog/2021/06/measuring-uncertainty-and-volatility-with-fred-data) distinguishes ex-post realized volatility from uncertainty and option-implied measures such as VIX, and illustrates frequency/axis/percentile choices.
- Other module references are embedded in `src/data/modules.json`, with source titles and URLs attached directly to each lesson.

## Teaching guardrails

- Each lesson separates description, prediction, inference, and causal claims; association is not proof of cause.
- Statistical significance is taught separately from effect size and practical/economic importance. A non-significant result is not proof of no effect.
- Financial examples are educational, often simulated or historical, and are not investment advice or forecasts.
- Interactive charts use deterministic browser calculations; AI answers receive the current module title, lesson text, and graph context as an explicit prompt preamble.

## Expanded applied-economics curriculum and gap-filling topics (42 modules, 238 lessons)

This project preserves its original 16 modules, lessons, illustrations and videos, and retains 14 original applied-economics modules. The current bundle has 42 modules and 238 lessons. Eight earlier additions beyond the 30-module catalogue cover multilevel/partial pooling, complex survey sampling, survival/duration analysis, spatial/regional econometrics, bootstrap inference, power and sample-size planning, measurement error, and price indices/real values. Four further modules fill distinct gaps in predictive regularization and cross-validation, economic evidence synthesis, causal mediation, and partial identification and sensitivity bounds. Two existing-module lessons distinguish survey sampling clusters from treatment-assignment clusters and explain how changing spatial shocks can persist in panels. New coverage extends the estimand, assumptions, local scope and inferential limits; references guide the curriculum, while all prose, examples, diagrams, labs and questions are newly written.

Chapter-specific labels link to the publisher record for [Angrist & Pischke, *Mostly Harmless Econometrics* (2008)](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691120355/mostly-harmless-econometrics): research design/experiments (Chs. 1–2), regression and omitted-variable bias (Ch. 3), IV/LATE (Ch. 4), panel/DiD (Ch. 5), RD (Ch. 6), quantile effects (Ch. 7), and clustered/serial-correlation inference (Ch. 8). ### Survival and duration analysis

- [UCLA OARC — Introduction to Survival Analysis in R](https://stats.oarc.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/survival_r_full.html) explains follow-up time, right-censoring, the survival and hazard functions, the Kaplan–Meier estimator, risk sets and Cox modeling.

### Figure review references and drawing rules

- The [Columbia Mailman School difference-in-differences overview](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/research/population-health-methods/difference-difference-estimation), Cunningham’s open [*Causal Inference: The Mixtape*, DiD chapter](https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences), and the [Effect Book's DiD chapter](https://theeffectbook.net/ch-DifferenceinDifference.html) informed the canonical two-group/two-period comparison: label both group means in both periods, show the treated group's post-treatment untreated potential outcome only as an unobserved counterfactual, compute the effect as (treated post − treated pre) − (comparison post − comparison pre), and state that its causal interpretation relies on parallel trends. Original diagrams are used; no source figure is copied.
- Penn State's [STAT 200 graphics lessons](https://online.stat.psu.edu/stat200/Lesson03.html) and the [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda31.htm) informed the review of quantitative plots: identify the measured quantity on each axis, show meaningful ticks and units where values are interpreted, label groups/series and uncertainty, and distinguish observed data from a teaching schematic or simulation. A plot without a quantitative scale is retained only when its purpose is explicitly conceptual (for example a DAG or flow diagram).
- The visual review also checks that marks encode the quantity described in the adjacent lesson and lab, that legends distinguish observed outcomes from model-based or counterfactual values, and that causal assumptions are named rather than implied by a decorative arrow. These rules guide this project's original schematics; they are not claims that every diagram must be a conventional chart.

### New interactive labs and editorial boundaries
The four new labs are local deterministic illustrations: normal-model shrinkage with known variance components; a two-group survey-weighting example plus an equal-size cluster design-effect approximation; exponential survival curves with a censoring display; and global Moran’s I on a synthetic 6×6 lattice. They make no live AI requests and do not treat simulated values as empirical findings. The labs intentionally distinguish survey representation weights from causal-treatment weights, partial-pooling models from robust standard errors, hazard ratios from risk differences, and spatial association from causal spillovers.

### Applied-statistics additions: resampling, planning, measurement and prices
- [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook — Bootstrap Plot](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/bootplot.htm) describes resampling observed units with replacement to estimate a statistic's sampling distribution and cautions that bootstrap methods are not suitable for every distribution or statistic. [Hesterberg (2015), “What Teachers Should Know About the Bootstrap”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4784504/) discusses bootstrap standard errors and intervals, dependence assumptions, and small-sample limitations. The module distinguishes ordinary row resampling from design-aware cluster or time-series resampling.
- [Penn State STAT ONLINE — Power Analysis](https://online.stat.psu.edu/statprogram/reviews/statistical-concepts/power-analysis) explains conditional power and sample-size planning. [NIST/SEMATECH — Sample Sizes Required for a Mean](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc222.htm) documents the role of significance level, type-II error, variability and effect size. The interactive curve uses a labeled normal-approximation design only; it is not a universal calculator for clustered or attriting studies.
- [United Nations Statistics Division — Measurement Error in Household Surveys](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/hhsurveys/pdf/chapter_9.pdf) reviews questionnaire, mode, interviewer and respondent sources of survey measurement error and approaches such as reinterviews, randomized survey treatments and record checks. [Regression and Other Stories — authors’ book site](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/regression/) provides further open chapter resources on measurement and regression. The lesson's attenuation statement is explicitly limited to classical additive error in a predictor.
